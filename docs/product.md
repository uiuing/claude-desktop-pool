# Product contract

Claude Pool is a native macOS menu bar companion with one visible pool of independently authenticated Claude subscriptions. The official Claude client owns authentication, inference, permissions, policy, and subscription enforcement. Pool owns local routing, process coordination, and a simple presentation.

## Daily experience

Pool runs as a regular desktop application with a Dock icon and an app-switcher entry. Its main window stays open when focus moves elsewhere. Closing the window takes Claude Pool out of the Dock and the app switcher and keeps it in the menu bar; opening the window from the menu, or opening the app again, brings its Dock icon back; the menu's Quit action requests normal application shutdown through the same checks as Command-Q. Quitting cancels unfinished new sign-ins and ends the pool's own Claude sessions with the client's normal exit; nobody has to end one by hand first, and no process is force-killed. A small panel shows that quitting is under way until it finishes or is refused. Installing a newer Claude Pool, however it is asked for, restarts it the same way once the replies Claude is still writing have finished, after a minute at most; meanwhile nothing new starts, the person can choose to restart at once, and a conversation waiting on a tool or its next message goes on after the restart where it was.

Open Pool; its managed official client installs by itself. Before the first account signs in, Pool says that Anthropic may suspend or ban an account used this way and goes on only when the person accepts; it is not said again beside accounts already in the pool or to sign one of them in again. Add an account through the official sign-in, then connect Desktop with one click: Pool writes its own gateway profile into Desktop's settings, records what was there before, and opens Desktop, or asks before restarting a running one. Accounts are identified by the email returned by Claude, never a user-entered alias. Each step is explicit and reversible. Thereafter Desktop connects to one local endpoint; accounts are selected behind that endpoint. Each piece of work is placed on its own: a new conversation, each of its subagents and each short task goes to the account best placed for it, weighing what each account's 5-hour, weekly and model limits have left, when they reset, what that kind of work was measured to cost there and how much the account already carries. A conversation's subagents run on different accounts at once rather than draining one. An existing conversation stays on its account for its prompt cache while that account can take its next turn; at a new user turn it moves to an account with more left only when staying would cost more than writing it whole elsewhere, and a long conversation, which costs more to move, stays longer. When every account is at its limit, Desktop is told when the first is back and the person gets a notification. There is no start or pause: once setup is complete the pool serves, and a refused quit returns to serving. Pool notices when Desktop's connection was changed elsewhere and offers Reconnect; Disconnect puts back Desktop's previous settings, keeping anything changed since. Connected Desktop starts in its third-party gateway mode, separate from its Anthropic sign-in data. A failed or limited account has a clear explanation. Pool does not change the user's normal Desktop login or existing Code sessions.

The compact dashboard shows the actual account or accounts serving current conversations, pool readiness, comparable-plan quota totals, source-reported quota windows, exact resets and countdowns, last observations and refresh state. Ultra and other plans need not have the same windows. Unknown, exhausted and explicitly non-applicable are distinct. Percentages from different plan capacities are not added. Reset times are official facts, never predictions. A dedicated Routing page shows live bindings, decision reasons, limits and the actual configured selection rules. The menu bar shows Claude's mark with one plan's 5-hour share left over its weekly share left (the user chooses the plan when there are several); when the plan has a model's own weekly limit, such as Fable's, the tightest of those stands beside them as a single figure ("F72%"). Its panel lists that plan's limits, then every account, most usable first: those that can serve now by headroom, unread ones, those at a limit by when they come back, those whose week is used up, then those that cannot be used. Every account counts in the totals (a duplicate sign-in is not a separate account), and the panel's count of usable, at-limit, unread and unavailable accounts adds up to all of them. An account whose week is used up, or that cannot be used (signed out, no subscription, needs attention, restricted by Anthropic), is not read by itself until its weekly reset or until it is fixed, so passing 5-hour resets, timed reads and failed-read retries do not keep trying it. Resets beyond tomorrow show their date as well as the weekday. A limit whose reset has passed counts as full again at once; the next reading confirms it. Readiness and totals are never filtered by model.

A model's own weekly limit, such as Fable's, is shown wherever the 5-hour and weekly limits are, with its reset, and holds back only that model: an account whose Fable limit is used up still serves Sonnet and Opus. The menu bar's lower figure is the plan's weekly limit, or a model's own weekly limit, led by its initial ("F12%"), when that has less left. Pool never agrees to usage credits: when Claude Code asks whether a model should go on usage credits, Pool closes the question unanswered and serves the request from another account.

Only accounts the pool is using are read by time, so a pool of a hundred accounts costs no more than a pool of three. An account not in use keeps its last reading: when a stated reset passes it counts as full at once and is read a minute later to confirm the new cycle; otherwise it is read only when a conversation starts on it with a reading over ten minutes old, when it is next in line as the account in use nears the switch threshold, or when the user asks. Opening the menu reads nothing; figures hours old are marked with their time. When work on an account stops, one more read records where it left it, and an account left open but unused is not read again. Active refresh changes continuously with measured model speed, turn frequency and concurrent work; it can target 10 seconds during a fast single conversation or parallel sessions. Waiting and recent work decay, and automatic periodic reads pause without recent activity. The pace also follows how fast each account's tightest limit nears the switch threshold, from 10 seconds to 10 minutes, and a frequently read account keeps its usage session between reads. Reset confirmation is always on: a reading that still shows the ended cycle is read again later, up to six times. Plan upgrades/downgrades and expired/revoked login have distinct states and native recovery actions. The default window is 680 by 560 points, with a 640 by 520 minimum. Detailed settings and route history scroll within that compact window.

The Usage page shows what the replies Pool served used: tokens, replies, cache hit rate and an estimated cost at API list prices, with what the cache saved, against the previous period. It breaks usage down by model, effort, plan and account, opens each model to the efforts its replies ran at, and narrows to any of them, and stays readable from one account to a hundred. These figures are local statistics, not quota or charges.

A limit starts on an account's first request after its reset. Pool can start limits itself, with one short request through the official client; this is off until the person turns it on in Settings, since it sends requests on their accounts. Set to the weekly limit, it starts each account's weekly limit at its reset, so the next weekly reset comes as early as it can; it can also start 5-hour limits at their resets, and each account's 5-hour limit at set times of day. Only an account that can serve is warmed, never one whose week is used up; a request that did not start the limit is tried again after 15 minutes, then twice as long each time, up to four hours, six times at most for one reset. While a set time is under five hours away, an idle 5-hour limit waits for it. Nothing is sent while the Mac sleeps or is offline. An account whose 5-hour limit is not running can also be started from the menu; while Pool starts 5-hour limits at their resets itself, the menu offers that only after its own try failed.

Desktop's own connection check (a single "." asking for one token) is answered by Pool without starting a session, once it has confirmed that an account could take a request; when none could, the check fails with the reason and Settings shows it. Desktop is shown the official client's model ids (`claude-sonnet-5-5`, …), each tagged with its tier, so it names them from its own catalog and offers their reasoning levels; when a session reports that an alias now runs on a newer model, Pool lists that id from then on.

Local sensitive data redaction is on by default, part of setup and required before forwarding work while it is on. The person can turn it off in Settings after confirming that new requests then go to Claude as written; the Settings row, the redaction sheet and the route view then say it is off, and turning it on again redacts from the next request, by rules alone until the model is ready. It uses AstrLink Guard with all supported categories enabled, and updates it by itself to the publisher's newer compatible releases; a failed update leaves the current version working. Rules and the local model inspect text and typed tool data; originals and reversible mappings stay in memory. Model readiness, current processing, per-run counts and a disposable preview appear in Settings, with the recent requests that had something redacted: what was received, what was sent instead and how many values were put back into the reply, side by side. That review lives in memory only, at most 40 requests with long text folded to the context around each replacement, and is gone when Pool quits or the person clears it. A full log, off by default and turned on in Settings after two confirmations, keeps everything Pool receives and sends, redaction originals included, on this Mac for the person's own analysis; it can be cleared there at any time. Image pixels are outside this text-detection scope. The routing animation shows actual protection and session activity without inventing flow while idle.

## Platform and scope

macOS 14+, SwiftUI/AppKit, English, Simplified Chinese, Traditional Chinese, Japanese and Korean. An original quiet visual style, keyboard access, accessible labels, compact menu panel, clear empty/setup/failure states, and an inert demo. Form labels align at the leading edge; selectors, switches and numeric controls share the trailing edge and a compact size. Numeric settings accept direct entry with units and range validation, and save only on explicit confirmation. Platform-neutral domain and protocol code leave room for another shell later.

Only the official interactive Claude Code backend is supported, driven in interactive mode. Input uses a private PTY. Standard MCP returns client tools to Desktop. The session's transcript, the reply-stream tap and the status line report results. Pool does not execute arbitrary Desktop tools itself, skip permissions, edit a transcript the client wrote, or fall back to print/API modes.

## Acceptance

The deliverable must build and package, pass protocol/routing/storage/process lifecycle tests on synthetic fixtures, and have visually inspected demo screenshots. A release additionally requires dedicated-account testing of official login isolation, the installed CLI hook contract, Desktop profile import, continued conversations, tools, cancellation, limits, recovery, sleep and restart. Signing/notarization and clean-machine testing are release gates, not properties proved by an ad-hoc local build. A known unverified gate must remain documented; no zero-defect guarantee is made.
